How this works — and what it can't do

Plain-English methodology. If anything here sounds like a guarantee of profit, re-read it — it isn't.

The site runs two independent layers — a price/maths layer and an analyst/football layer — that only meet at the output. Tap a source, a layer, a tab, or a relationship chip below to read precisely what it does and how it links onward.

1. Where the prices come from

Live decimal odds for every fixture in the Premier League, La Liga and the Scottish Premiership, from The Odds API, covering the Irish-accessible bookmakers (Paddy Power, Betfair, Bet365, BoyleSports, Ladbrokes, William Hill). The timestamp in the header is the truth about freshness — odds are cached and refreshed on a schedule, so they are minutes-to-hours old, not live ticks. Always confirm the price at the book before you stake.

2. "Fair" probability — de-vigging

Bookmaker odds include a built-in margin (the "vig"): the implied probabilities of Home/Draw/Away sum to more than 100%. We remove it proportionally for each bookmaker, then average across books to get a consensus fair probability for each outcome. Two honest limits:

  • "Fair" means the market average, not the truth. Beating it means you got a better price than the consensus — it does not prove the consensus is right.
  • Proportional de-vigging slightly flatters longshots (the favourite–longshot bias). Small edges on big prices are therefore treated as noise and labelled speculative, not value.

3. Exchange commission — why the best price isn't the best price

Betting exchanges (Betfair, Smarkets) post the best raw price on almost every outcome, so they win a naive best-price comparison nearly every time. But an exchange takes commission on your net winnings — typically 2–5%. The price on screen is not the price you collect.

On a market where genuine edges are around 1%, that commission is the whole edge. So exchange prices here are converted to what you actually keep — net = 1 + (price − 1) × (1 − commission) — before any edge is calculated and before any book is declared best. A card backed by an exchange states both the shown price and the net price.

The default assumes 2%. If your rate differs, the deployment can set EXCHANGE_COMMISSION. This is also why accumulators never use exchange prices: you cannot place an acca on an exchange at all.

4. Edge and EV

Edge = fair probability − the probability implied by the best price (in percentage points). It's a model diagnostic. The money number is EV (expected value): EV = fair probability × price − 1. "EV +€0.30 per €10" means that if this exact bet were repeated many times and the fair probability is correct, you'd average +€0.30 profit per €10 staked. Any single bet still just wins or loses — EV only shows up over dozens of bets. Variance dominates small samples.

5. The sharp-line cross-check

Not all bookmakers are equally informed. Pinnacle and the betting exchanges run margins around 2% (against 5–8% at a typical high-street book), accept large stakes, and move their prices first when real money arrives. Their de-vigged line is the closest thing available to a true probability.

Every outcome is therefore compared against that sharp line as well as against the soft-book consensus. When the sharp book rates an outcome more likely than the consensus does, the sharp money agrees it is underpriced — that is confirmation. When the sharp book is shorter, the apparent edge is more likely the model being wrong than a real opportunity, and the card says so.

This replaced a prediction-market check (Kalshi/Polymarket) that the World Cup version of this app used. Those venues price big tournaments but barely cover a midweek league fixture, so for club football they were empty far more often than not.

6. The AI analyst

For each fixture the app runs one web search across trusted football sources for that league (Sky Sports, ESPN, the official league sites, FotMob and similar), gathering recent form, league position, confirmed team news and head-to-head. A language-model analyst then writes the football read from that material and nothing else.

It is explicitly constrained: it may not invent a scoreline, a table position or a statistic; it may not name a manager or a signing it is not confident about; and — the rule that matters most in a betting product — it may not read "no injuries found" as "the squad is fit". Missing information is reported as missing, never as good news.

It never sees enough to judge value. It supplies football reasons; the price layer judges the odds. It is a second opinion, not a fact, and it can be wrong.

7. Best Bets — the two gates

A bet appears only when (1) the analyst explicitly recommended that outcome for football reasons, and (2) the current price is fair or better for that pick. Verdicts: Bet Now (EV ≥ +2% and outside the longshot-noise band), Lean (roughly fair price), Track Price (analyst likes it, price short), Avoid at Price.

8. Accumulators are −EV. Full stop.

Multiplying legs multiplies the bookmaker's margin. The acca tools here exist because people enjoy accas: they find the least-bad version (single-book pricing, high-probability legs, honest EV in red). The long-term expectation of every acca on this site is negative and the cards say exactly how negative. The genuine edge, where it exists, is in singles.

9. What would prove this works

A logged record. The My Bets journal records the model's fair probability and EV at the moment you log a bet, so over time you can compare expected P/L with actual P/L and check whether prices you took beat the market's later prices (closing line value). Until that record exists over a meaningful sample, treat every number here as a hypothesis, not a promise.

10. Bankroll

Flat, small stakes — 1–2% of a bankroll you can afford to lose — survive variance. Chasing losses and increasing stakes after wins are both how positive-EV bettors go broke. If betting stops being fun, stop.
